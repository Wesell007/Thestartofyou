### Key factual points supported

- Itching in pregnancy is common and usually not caused by anything serious; it can be caused by hormone changes and skin stretching, and usually stops after birth (NHS).
- It can sometimes be caused by intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP), a liver condition that can be serious and needs treating with medicine (NHS).
- ICP is the current UK name; it was previously called obstetric cholestasis (RCOG GTG 43).
- Self-care supported by NHS: pat or tap rather than scratch; cool or warm rather than hot baths or showers; unperfumed moisturiser or emollient; short nails; loose natural-fibre clothing; no more than 20 minutes in the bath or shower; avoid perfumed soaps, deodorants and moisturisers; go easy on very spicy food and caffeinated drinks.
- A pharmacist can suggest creams, lotions and pregnancy-safe antihistamines, and can say when to see a GP or midwife (NHS).
- GP or midwife treatments may include antihistamine tablets, emollients and steroid cream; onward referral to a dermatologist or maternity unit is possible (NHS).
- If ICP is diagnosed, a care plan is agreed, extra appointments are offered to check on parent and baby, and medicines are offered to treat the liver and reduce itching (NHS).
- With ICP there is a small risk of the baby being born before 37 weeks, and a small risk of stillbirth if ICP is severe; the care team discusses risk individually; early induction may be suggested; most people with ICP make a full recovery and have a healthy birth (NHS).

### Safety/escalation points

Non-urgent — see a GP if pregnant with itchy skin and: it is affecting daily life; it does not get better after pharmacist treatment or keeps returning; you have eczema (which can worsen in pregnancy); you have a new rash, lump or swelling (NHS).

Urgent — get urgent medical help now if pregnant and: the itching is very itchy, for example keeping you awake at night; the palms of the hands or soles of the feet are itchy; the itching is worse at night. Call your maternity unit if you have the number; if you cannot contact them, call 111 (NHS).

This wording is unambiguous in the current NHS page and will be carried across without softening. The article will not tell anyone to wait and see when these features are present.

### Claims intentionally excluded

- Any prevalence figure for ICP, any probability that itching means ICP, and any numeric stillbirth or preterm risk. NHS states "small risk" without a figure for the reader; no fetched UK source gives a patient-facing number.
- Bile acid thresholds, liver function test values, and severity classification. Clinician-facing detail from GTG 43; not appropriate patient-facing content here.
- Named medicine dosing, including ursodeoxycholic acid regimens.
- Timing of induction in ICP as a rule. NHS says it may be suggested; the decision is individual.
- Anything about pruritic urticarial papules and plaques of pregnancy (PUPPP/PEP) or pemphigoid gestationis — no UK source was fetched for these, so they are out.

| NHS | Caesarean section — Recovery (page last reviewed 4 January 2023, next review 4 January 2026) | https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/caesarean-section/recovery/ | 9 Sep 2026 | Hospital stay, wound care, scar, pain relief, bleeding, returning to activity, when to get medical advice |

### Key factual points supported

- A caesarean is an operation to deliver a baby through a cut in the tummy and womb, usually across the lower tummy just below the bikini line; it is major surgery and is generally done when it is the safest option (NHS).
- Around 45% of babies born in England are delivered by caesarean (NHS).
- It may be planned (elective) or carried out in an emergency; planned caesareans are usually done from the 39th week (NHS).
- Reasons include breech position, low-lying placenta, pre-eclampsia, certain infections, concerns about the baby's oxygen and nutrients, labour not progressing, or heavy bleeding (NHS).
- Someone can request a caesarean without a medical reason; they should be offered a discussion of benefits and risks and support for birth anxiety, and if they still feel a vaginal birth is not acceptable they should be offered a planned caesarean, with referral if their doctor is unwilling (NHS).
- Most caesareans are under spinal or epidural anaesthetic, so the person is awake with the lower body numbed; a screen is placed across the body; a cut about 10 to 20cm is usually made; tugging and pulling may be felt; the baby can usually be seen and held straight away if well; the operation normally takes about 40 to 50 minutes; general anaesthetic is occasionally used (NHS).
- Recovery usually takes longer than after a vaginal birth; most people go home 1 to 2 days afterwards; painkillers are offered; driving and some activities may wait until after the 6-week postnatal check; the scar fades with time (NHS).
- In hospital: painkillers, close contact with the baby and breastfeeding can start, early mobilising is encouraged, eating and drinking when hungry or thirsty, a catheter for at least 12 hours, dressing for at least 24 hours; non-dissolvable stitches or staples usually removed by the midwife after 5 to 7 days (NHS).
- Paracetamol or ibuprofen are usually the preferred painkillers while breastfeeding; aspirin and codeine (in co-codamol) are not usually recommended when breastfeeding (NHS).
- Vaginal bleeding can occur; use pads not tampons; get advice if bleeding is heavy (NHS).
- Possible complications: wound or womb-lining infection, blood clots, heavy bleeding, damage to nearby areas such as the bladder or ureters, temporary breathing difficulties in the baby, and accidental cuts to the baby (NHS).
- A caesarean does not mean future babies must be born the same way; most people who have had a caesarean can safely have a vaginal birth next time (VBAC), sometimes with extra monitoring; some are advised to have another caesarean (NHS).

### Safety/escalation points

Contact your midwife or GP straight away after a caesarean if you have severe pain, leaking urine, pain when peeing, heavy vaginal bleeding, a wound that becomes more red, painful or swollen, pus or foul-smelling fluid from the wound, a cough or shortness of breath, or swelling or pain in your lower leg — these can be signs of infection or a blood clot (NHS).

### Claims intentionally excluded

- Numeric risk rates for individual complications; no patient-facing figures were fetched.
- Anything about surgical technique beyond what NHS describes to patients.
- Individual eligibility for VBAC, or advice on choosing between birth routes.
- Detailed rehabilitation, scar-tissue work, later scar sensation and long-term recovery — reserved for Phase 32C.
- Local variation in enhanced recovery pathways; not fetched, so described only as "practice varies between trusts" without specifics.

## Gestational diabetes

### Search intent

Phase 31 cluster C037, priority P0, example keyword "gestational diabetes" (27,100). Intent is condition-first: what it is, what it means for me and the baby, what happens after a diagnosis. Phase 31 records the test as already covered and the condition as not.

### Existing Start of You coverage

`glucose-tolerance-test` owns how and when the test works, and `tests-and-scans-in-pregnancy` covers screening context and risk factors. Nothing owns the condition itself or life after diagnosis. Confirmed partial gap with MEDIUM cannibalisation risk.

### UK authoritative sources

| Organisation | Title | URL | Checked | Claims supported |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NHS | Gestational diabetes — Overview (page last reviewed 8 December 2022; stated next review 8 December 2025 has passed, page still live) | https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/gestational-diabetes/ | 9 Sep 2026 | Definition, risk factors, symptoms, effects on pregnancy, screening, treatment overview, birth timing, postnatal follow-up, future pregnancies |
| NICE | NG3 Diabetes in pregnancy: management from preconception to the postnatal period (published 2015, last updated 16 December 2020, last reviewed 30 April 2025) | https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng3 | 9 Sep 2026 | Existence and scope of the UK care pathway: diagnosis, antenatal, intrapartum, neonatal and postnatal care including ongoing blood glucose testing |

### Key factual points supported

- Gestational diabetes is high blood glucose that develops in pregnancy and usually goes away after birth; it can happen at any stage but is more common in the second or third trimester; it happens when the body cannot make enough insulin for the extra needs of pregnancy (NHS).
- It can cause problems for parent and baby, and those risks can be reduced when it is picked up early and well managed (NHS).
- Risk factors: age over 40; BMI above 30; a previous baby weighing 4.5kg or more; gestational diabetes in a previous pregnancy; a parent or sibling with diabetes; south Asian, Black, African-Caribbean or Middle Eastern origin; previous gastric bypass or other weight-loss surgery. Anyone with one or more of these should be offered screening (NHS).
- It usually causes no symptoms and is most often found through screening; if blood glucose gets high, symptoms can include increased thirst, needing to pee more often, dry mouth, tiredness, blurred vision and genital itching or thrush — many of which are common in pregnancy anyway (NHS).
- Most people with gestational diabetes have otherwise normal pregnancies and healthy babies (NHS).
- Possible problems: a larger baby, which can make birth harder and makes induction or caesarean more likely; polyhydramnios; premature birth; pre-eclampsia; low blood sugar or jaundice in the baby after birth; and, rarely, stillbirth (NHS).
- Screening is by oral glucose tolerance test, usually at 24 to 28 weeks, earlier if there was gestational diabetes in a previous pregnancy (NHS). Detail stays on the existing testing article.
- Treatment: a blood glucose testing kit to monitor; changes to diet and activity where possible; medicine — tablets or insulin injections — if those changes are not enough; closer monitoring through pregnancy and birth (NHS).
- Birth: it is best to give birth before 41 weeks, and induction or caesarean may be recommended if labour has not started by then; earlier birth may be recommended if there are concerns or glucose has not been well controlled (NHS).
- Afterwards: a blood test to check for diabetes 6 to 13 weeks after birth and once a year after that if normal; higher chance of gestational diabetes again and of type 2 diabetes later; see a GP without waiting for the next test if symptoms of high blood glucose appear (NHS).
- The NHS Diabetes Prevention Programme supports people with a history of gestational diabetes, including by self-referral (NHS).
- If planning another pregnancy after gestational diabetes, ask a GP to check for diabetes first (NHS).
- The UK care pathway is set out in NICE NG3 and spans diagnosis, antenatal, intrapartum, neonatal and postnatal care (NICE).

### Safety/escalation points

Speak to your midwife or doctor about any symptoms that worry you (NHS). After birth, see a GP without waiting for the scheduled test if symptoms of high blood glucose appear (NHS). Anyone already diagnosed is under a maternity and diabetes team and should use the contact route that team gives them; the article says that rather than inventing a universal number.

### Claims intentionally excluded

- All glucose target values and diagnostic thresholds. NG3 carries them, but they are clinician-facing, are applied individually, and were not fetched at recommendation level; presenting them would risk personalised targets.
- Named medicines and doses, including metformin regimens.
- Any diet plan, carbohydrate guidance, meal suggestion or weight advice.
- Prevalence figures and any statement of how likely a given risk factor makes diagnosis. Risk factors are described as reasons screening is offered, never as predictions.
- Local pathway specifics — monitoring frequency, clinic structure, growth scan schedules — which vary by trust and were not fetched.

### Cannibalisation check

Risk MEDIUM, managed by a hard intent boundary. `glucose-tolerance-test` keeps how and when the test works, including fasting and the drink. `tests-and-scans-in-pregnancy` keeps the screening overview. The new article owns what the condition is, what happens after diagnosis, and what care involves, and links to the testing article rather than restating it. No new page for keyword variants such as "gestational diabetes diet" or "GD test results".

## Evidence conflicts / uncertainties

1. The NHS gestational diabetes page states a next review date of 8 December 2025, which has passed, while the page remains live and is the current NHS patient-facing source. No contradicting NHS or NICE material was found. Handled by keeping claims general and pathway-level and excluding all numeric targets. Flagged for human reviewer confirmation.
2. NHS caesarean recovery states a next review date of 4 January 2026, also passed, page still live. Same handling.
3. NICE NG3 carries an active notice about insulin detemir discontinuation and a pending update relating to hybrid closed loop systems. Neither affects any claim used here, because no medicine or device claim is made. Recorded for transparency.
4. RCOG GTG 43 (2022) is clinician-facing and was used only for terminology and for the principle that diagnosis and risk assessment are clinician-led. No clinical parameter was taken from it.
5. NHS states the caesarean rate as "around 45% of babies born in England". This is England-specific and is presented as such, not as a UK figure.

None of these conflicts blocks drafting. No claim was filled from general model knowledge.
