## Expansion 1 — `healing-after-birth`: bleeding after birth (C080)

**Current owner:** `/first-year/postpartum-recovery/healing-after-birth` — First Year dataset, `status: "ready"`, `medicallyReviewed: true`, `reviewedBy: "Jenny Joines"`.

**Intent not currently covered:** The existing "Bleeding, soreness and tenderness" section says bleeding is expected, eases, changes colour, and that pads suit better than tampons. It does not name lochia, does not describe the pattern in sequence, does not explain the tampon advice, and names no red flags. C080 is P0 and safety-critical.

**Proposed change:** Split the existing section into two. Keep the soreness content where it is; replace the bleeding paragraph with a dedicated bleeding section, and extend the existing "When to ask for advice" section with named urgent signs.

### Proposed replacement section — "Bleeding after birth"

Heading: Bleeding after birth

Body paragraph 1: Bleeding from the vagina after birth is expected, and it has a name you may hear from your midwife: lochia. It is heaviest in the first days, so super-absorbent maternity pads are worth having more of than you think you need. Over the following weeks the flow gradually settles and the colour turns brownish before it stops altogether.

Body paragraph 2: If you are breastfeeding, you may notice the bleeding becomes redder or heavier while you feed, sometimes with period-like cramps. That is your womb contracting, and it is a normal part of the process rather than a setback.

Body paragraph 3: Use pads rather than tampons, menstrual cups or other internal products until after your postnatal check. While the place where your placenta was attached is still healing, anything internal carries an infection risk. This applies whether you had a vaginal birth or a caesarean.

### Proposed additions to "When to ask for advice"

Add, keeping the existing calm framing:

- Tell your midwife if you are losing blood in large clots, as you may need treatment.
- Contact your midwife, health visitor or GP straight away if bleeding suddenly becomes very heavy, or if you feel faint or your heart is racing.
- Contact them the same day if you have a high temperature alongside a sore, tender tummy, which can mean an infection.
- Get urgent help for pain, swelling or redness in one calf, or for chest pain or difficulty breathing.
- Get urgent help for a headache with vision changes and vomiting.

### Content to preserve

Every existing section, the calm register, the both-birth-routes framing, the existing key takeaways and the existing source list, which already includes the NHS "Your body after the birth" page used for this expansion.

### Content to avoid

Millilitre thresholds, pad-count self-diagnosis rules, any promise that bleeding stops by a fixed week, and any wording that softens the urgent signs.

**Search-intent effect:** Lets this page properly own the lochia and postnatal-bleeding cluster instead of the intent scattering across a general recovery page. No new URL, so no cannibalisation of `/articles/postpartum-recovery-timeline`.

**Related internal links to add:** the proposed perineal healing page; `postnatal-checks-and-appointments`; `when-to-ask-for-help-after-birth`.

**Review classification:** SAFETY_REVIEW_REQUIRED.

## Expansion 2 — `body-changes-after-birth`: pelvic floor, bladder and bowel (C046)

**Current owner:** `/first-year/body-and-hormones/body-changes-after-birth` — First Year dataset, `status: "ready"`, `medicallyReviewed: true`, `reviewedBy: "Jenny Joines"`.

**Intent not currently covered:** Postnatal pelvic floor, leaking, piles and bowel changes. The strong exercise page, `/articles/pelvic-floor-exercises-in-pregnancy`, is framed for pregnancy and does not carry the after-birth context. A separate postnatal pelvic-floor page would compete with it directly, so the intent is served by an expansion plus a link.

**Proposed change:** Add one new section, placed after the existing body-change content.

### Proposed new section — "Your pelvic floor, bladder and bowel"

Heading: Your pelvic floor, bladder and bowel

Body paragraph 1: Leaking a little pee when you laugh, cough, sneeze or move suddenly is quite common after birth, and so is a heavy or dragging feeling low down. Your pelvic floor has carried a great deal, and like any other muscle it takes time and gentle use to come back.

Body paragraph 2: Pelvic floor exercises are the thing that helps most. Sit or lie comfortably, squeeze and draw in as though you are holding in wind, then squeeze as though you are stopping the flow of pee. Mix long squeezes, held for up to ten seconds, with quick short ones, and build towards ten of each, three times a day. Keep breathing normally and do not pull your stomach in.

Body paragraph 3: Bowel changes are just as common and just as rarely mentioned. Piles often turn up after birth and usually settle within days or weeks; your midwife or pharmacist can suggest something soothing. Constipation is worth heading off with fibre and fluids, since straining makes piles worse, and a gentle laxative is fine if you need one.

Body paragraph 4: If pelvic floor exercises are not helping, say so at your postnatal check. Your GP can refer you to a physiotherapist, and that referral exists precisely for this. Tell your midwife or GP if you are leaking poo, pooing without meaning to, or struggling to hold in wind or pee, and get advice if you have real difficulty passing urine. None of this is something you are meant to simply live with, and you can ask for help at any point rather than waiting for an appointment.

### Content to preserve

The existing sections, key takeaways, sources and the article's warm, non-judgemental tone about bodies after birth.

### Content to avoid

Cure timelines, product or pelvic-floor-trainer recommendations, prolapse self-diagnosis, and any suggestion that leaking is simply part of motherhood.

**Search-intent effect:** Gives postnatal pelvic floor, bladder and bowel queries a home inside First Year while leaving the strong pregnancy exercise page as the owner of the exercise keyword itself. Prevents fragmentation into separate leaking, piles and constipation pages.

**Related internal links to add:** `/articles/pelvic-floor-exercises-in-pregnancy` for the exercise detail; the proposed separated tummy muscles page; `postnatal-checks-and-appointments`.

**Review classification:** HEALTH_REVIEW_REQUIRED.
